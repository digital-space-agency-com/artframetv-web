How to turn any TV into a digital photo frame

A television is a far better digital photo frame than most digital photo frames — it is bigger, it is already on the wall, and it is already paid for. What stops it looking like one is usually brightness and framing, not resolution.

Updated 20 October 2026 For Google TV & Android TV

The four approaches

  1. The TV's built-in ambient mode. Easiest, no installation. Google TV and most manufacturer skins have one. Limited to the sources the maker allows, and generally requires a connection.
  2. A dedicated app. Full control over framing, interval and sources, and it can store images locally so it works offline. Needs a one-off setup.
  3. A USB stick. Most TVs will run a slideshow from a USB drive with no network at all. Crude, no matting, but completely private and offline.
  4. Casting from a phone. Fine for showing someone holiday photos; useless as a permanent frame, since it ties up the phone.

The settings that matter more than the app

This is where most setups fail. A TV is lit from behind and ships configured to look bright in a showroom. Real artwork and real prints reflect light, they do not emit it.

  • Backlight 25–35%. The single biggest change. A dim screen reads as paper.
  • Picture mode: Warm, or Filmmaker Mode. Kills the blue cast that makes a screen look like a screen.
  • Reduce contrast slightly. Lowers digital glare.
  • Turn off motion smoothing. Irrelevant for still images but it sometimes introduces artefacts on slow pans.

Framing, and why letterboxing ruins it

Photographs are usually 3:2 or 4:3. Televisions are 16:9. Something has to give. Filling the screen crops the picture; fitting it leaves black bars, which reads unmistakably as "a screen showing an image".

The third option is to treat the leftover area as mount board rather than dead space — a passe-partout, the way a framer would. A bevelled mat with a visible cut edge, slightly wider at the bottom than the top, is what makes the eye read "framed print" instead of "letterbox".

Burn-in, if you have an OLED

A static image held for hours is the one thing OLED panels genuinely dislike. Any app you use for this should either move the image slightly over time or change it often enough that no edge sits on the same pixels. Combined with a low backlight — which you want anyway — the risk is small.

Common questions

Can I use my smart TV as a digital picture frame?
Yes. Use the TV's built-in ambient mode, a dedicated photo frame app, or a USB slideshow. The app route gives the most control and can work offline.
What is the best TV setting for displaying photos?
Drop the backlight to 25–35%, set the picture mode to Warm or Filmmaker Mode, and reduce contrast slightly. Real artwork does not emit light, and lowering brightness is what completes the illusion.
Will displaying a photo for hours burn my OLED TV?
It is the main risk with static images. Use an app that drifts the image slightly or changes it regularly, and keep the backlight low. Both together make burn-in unlikely.
How do I stop black bars around photos on my TV?
Either crop to 16:9 and lose part of the composition, or mat the image so the leftover screen becomes a deliberate mount board instead of a black bar.
